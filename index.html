Why we built it

  • The Released Date column was filled by hand — one provider site per game, every week.
  • Other teams use that sheet. A wrong date spreads.

Why we built it

  • Speed complaints arrived as chat messages. No video, no date, no number.
  • Developers could not act on them, so most were never fixed.
  • We needed the same test, every week, in the same shape, with evidence attached.

What it does

  • A video is attached to every test. The record is evidence, not an opinion.
  • Lag is tagged against a spin range, then rolled up into a smooth rate and a grade.

  • Coverage today: TH and PH; PG Soft and Jili.

Result

Measured result Testing from W2 to W10 found lag on PG Soft and Jili titles. We passed it to the developers. It was fixed, deployed, and TH has been stable since.

Open issues

Lesson: attach evidence to a subjective report and it becomes actionable. We applied the same rule to the other tools.

Why we built it

  • Finding games with no icon meant reading every tab of the weekly QA sheet by eye.
  • The sheet keeps growing, so the job got slower every month.
  • Worst part: a missed row looked the same as no rows.

What it does

  • A script reads every visible tab of the weekly Game / QA production test sheet, finds rows flagged as having no icon, and writes them to the icon scouting sheet.
  • Output columns match the live template. A provenance column records which tab each row came from.
  • It reports its own detection, separating "no flagged rows" from "header not found" from "tab hidden".
  • It installs as a menu item on the sheet. Operators never touch the script.
  • The tool: named sign-in for 11 DX and management users, timestamps in GMT+7, icons processed in batches.

Result

In weekly use since 7 Jul 2026. The scan is one menu click with a full audit trail, and a detection failure is now visible instead of silent.

Why we built it

  • Competitor scouting has to answer one question: is this domain actually licensed in this market?
  • A site showing a regulator logo or a licence number is not proof. Only presence on the official register is.
  • The registers are scattered, in different formats, and some regulators publish no domain search at all.

What it does

  • Enter a domain, pick a market: PH (PAGCOR), MX (SEGOB), TH, BD.
  • Checks embedded official registers automatically — PAGCOR 356 domains (18 Mar 2025, mirror domains included), UKGC 1,378, Isle of Man 52 across 54 licensees, Kahnawake 195, plus the MGA 297-entry confirmed-fraud blacklist.
  • Licence text check: paste the licensing text a site shows in its footer or T&Cs, and it flags contradictory, defunct or unverifiable regulator claims.
  • Gibraltar by company name, because Gibraltar publishes 58 licensed companies by legal name and no domain list.
  • Links straight out to the registers that have no usable search: SEGOB, Curaçao, the MGA URL checker, Anjouan.

Result

In monthly use for competitor scouting. Its real value is the regulator knowledge written into it, which a person checking by hand would miss:

  • Curaçao reformed on 24 Dec 2024, so the old master-licence numbers are defunct — and there is no domain search: a certificate is only reachable from the seal on the operator's own site.
  • Several sites impersonate the Anjouan regulator. The tool links only the genuine register, and says that even a real match means lighter oversight than the other regulators.
  • Kahnawake's list is dated 28 Sep 2023 and may be stale. The tool states that on screen rather than implying it is current.
Not measured. No run counter. And the register snapshots are dated — each source shows its own date on screen, but they need refetching periodically to stay accurate.

Why we built it

  • The catalogue holds 52,833 scraped rows — 52,074 once duplicate crawls are set aside — across 1,336 game providers, alongside the 47 competitor sites DX tracks in four markets.
  • Every attribute column is text, and empty values were filled with junk rather than left blank. Only 56.9% of the RTP column holds a readable percentage.
  • A straightforward average over that column either errors, or silently averages 57% of the data and presents it as the whole.
  • A number pulled from this catalogue has to arrive with its denominator attached, or it cannot be defended a week after it was quoted.

What it does

  • Ask in ordinary words. It works out the SQL, runs it read-only, and returns the number together with the coverage figure that qualifies it — both measured in the same query, so the caveat cannot drift from the figure.
  • Lists the tables, row counts and live dimension values before answering, so a new table, a widened scrape or a half-finished load is caught rather than averaged over.
  • Picks the table whose grain matches the question. One row per game is not one row per site.
  • Applies the known traps: junk values, case differences between tables, and columns that hold a single value and can answer nothing.
  • Answers number first, then coverage, then the table it read and the date it read it.

What it will not do

  • It refuses rather than approximates. The catalogue is a single snapshot, so trend and growth questions get a refusal, not an estimate.
  • Release-date questions are refused while the source column mixes date formats with spreadsheet serials.
  • A market that has not been scraped returns a stated absence, not a zero that reads like an empty market.
  • The games and the tracked competitors are two different populations — the games come from a public industry aggregator, not from the 47 sites — so "which games does competitor X offer" has no answer here, and it says so instead of joining them.

Result

Worked example Asked which tracked competitors are IP-restricted, it returned 7 of 47 — six recorded one way in the source, one recorded another, matched on meaning rather than exact text — and showed the buckets reconciling to the full 47, so nothing had been quietly dropped.

Open issues

  • A review against live data found six defects in the documented query patterns: two error out, three return a silently wrong number, one makes a broken setup look healthy. Patching them is open work.
  • The figures above are a snapshot dated 11 Sep 2026 of a catalogue that reloads.
  • No unique constraint on the game link column, so every reload re-admits the duplicates — currently 1.4% of rows, stamped with different page numbers by a source that re-paginated mid-crawl.
  • One market is crawled. The other three tracked markets have no catalogue data, and no load history exists, so no trend question can ever be answered from this snapshot.

Why we built it

  • Every request made to DX lands in one log. Rows are created from Slack reactions — a ticket reaction opens a row, a tick marks it shipped. Nothing is typed by hand.
  • That log is department-wide, so anyone working in Automation & Workflow had to filter past Game Ops, Game Inventory and Market Advantages work every time.

What it does

  • Three linked views of the same data source, each filtered to the A&W pillar. Nothing is duplicated — no sync job, no copied rows, no second source of truth.
  • Open — the daily working list. P0 at the top, and within a priority the oldest unanswered request sits above newer ones.
  • By status — a board. Open against Released at a glance.
  • All requests — the full history, including released date and the deliverable that closed each one out.
  • A row created by the Slack reaction appears in both places at once, and a row edited in either place is the same row.

Result

In active use. It sits beside the DX Request Log in the shared docs database rather than underneath it, so it is discoverable the same way the rest of the DX documentation is. The same pattern rebuilds any other slice of the log — by pillar, by type, by owner — because the filter is the only thing that differs.

Open work. The filter is exact, so A&W work logged under the plain DX tag does not appear here; correcting the tags at source is cleaner than widening the filter, which would pull in genuine non-A&W work. The Type options are inherited placeholders, so any breakdown by Type is indicative until a real taxonomy replaces them.

What it does

  • Crawls competitor sites and reads the popular-games list each one publishes.
  • Covers all regions and both global and local competitors.
  • Compiles the results into a chart of the games currently popular in a region.
  • Takes data from more than one route: AI crawl, Excel input, and other feeds.

Where it is

  • Still in the development phase. The DX team is testing it now.
  • It runs on the internal network behind a sign-in, so it is not reachable from the open internet.
  • Nothing about it is finished enough to quote numbers from yet.

Why it matters

Knowing which games are popular on competitor sites, per region, currently means checking sites by hand. This turns that into one compiled chart per region, and it accepts data from whichever route is available rather than depending on the crawl alone.

Before you open it: it is on the internal network, so it needs VPN, and it uses a self-signed certificate — the browser will show a security warning before the sign-in prompt. Both are expected for a pilot on an internal host.

Why we built it

  • Onboarding was a link to the Notion workspace. Every new joiner chose their own reading order.
  • The S5 Operating System holds 330 documents. No order, no priority, no record of what was read.
  • Two costs: new joiners took longer to become useful, and managers could not tell who had read what.

What it does

  • Sign in with an @s5tech.co email and a code issued per person.
  • Pick your team, get 4 weeks. W1 company picture, same for everyone. W2 all team missions plus your team's direction, frameworks and career ladder. W3 your team's SOPs and scope. W4 neighbour teams, performance, growth.

How it plays

  • 20 fixed paylines. Match 3, 4 or 5 from the leftmost reel.
  • Knockout: a KO symbol expands to fill its whole reel and substitutes for any paying symbol. All three fighter portraits count as one Fighter symbol. Bells are never substituted.
  • Championship rounds: 3+ bells before wild expansion award 8 free spins. Every 2 spins is a boxing round. No retriggers.
  • Road to Gold: each crest on a paid spin adds a point. 0–5 → 1× bonus wins, 6–11 → 2×, 12 → 3×. The level locks when a bonus starts, then the meter resets.
  • Try Bonus previews the feature at no credit cost, leaving balance and bet untouched. A reviewer sees the bonus without grinding for it.

Result

The process ran again and produced a harder game: layered mechanics, a market theme, and a bonus preview so reviewers see the feature in seconds instead of spinning for it.

Stated in the game itself
  • Free play, virtual credits, no cash prizes.
  • Medium volatility is the design target. RTP and volatility are not certified.
  • Road to Gold is labelled an in-session mechanic, not a loyalty programme, so it cannot be mistaken for a live promotion.
  • Session resets on refresh.
